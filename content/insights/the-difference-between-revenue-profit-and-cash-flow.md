"We had our best year ever, so why is money so tight?" It's one of the most common — and most stressful — questions in growing businesses. The answer almost always comes down to confusing three different numbers: revenue, profit and cash flow.

They're related, but they measure very different things. Here's a plain-language guide. (This is general education, not accounting or financial advice — for decisions specific to your business, speak with a qualified accountant.)

## Revenue: what you sold

Revenue is the total value of what you sold in a period — before any costs are taken out. It's the headline number, and it's the one most people celebrate.

Revenue tells you about demand. It does not tell you whether the business is healthy.

## Profit: what's left after costs

Profit is what remains after you subtract the costs of running the business. It's useful to think about it in two layers:

- **Gross profit** — revenue minus the direct costs of delivering what you sold (materials, direct labour, production).
- **Net profit** — what's left after *all* costs, including rent, salaries, marketing, software and interest.

A business can have impressive revenue and very thin profit if its costs grow as fast as its sales — or faster.

## Cash flow: when money actually moves

Cash flow is about *timing*: when money actually enters and leaves your bank account. And this is where profitable businesses get into trouble.

Profit is often recorded when you make a sale. Cash arrives when the customer actually pays. Meanwhile, you may have paid suppliers, staff and rent weeks or months earlier.

## A simple illustration

Imagine (with round, illustrative numbers) a business that wins a large project worth 10 lakh rupees, with costs of 7 lakh. On paper, that's a 3 lakh profit. Good news.

But suppose:

- The business pays its team and suppliers the 7 lakh over the next two months.
- The client pays on 90-day terms.

For three months, the business is 7 lakh *out of pocket* on a profitable project. If it wins two more projects like this at the same time, the cash gap triples — even as revenue and profit look better than ever. That's how growth itself can create a cash crunch.

## Questions every owner should be able to answer

1. **What are our gross margins** on each main product or service?
2. **How long does it take customers to pay** us, on average?
3. **How quickly do we have to pay** suppliers and staff?
4. **How many months of fixed costs** could we cover with the cash we have today?
5. **What does our cash position look like** over the next 13 weeks?

You don't need to be an accountant to track these. A simple spreadsheet, reviewed monthly with whoever does your books, is enough to stay ahead of most surprises.

## Practical ways to improve cash flow

- Ask for deposits or staged payments on larger projects.
- Shorten payment terms where you can, and follow up on invoices promptly.
- Negotiate longer terms with suppliers where it's fair to do so.
- Be careful about growing faster than your cash can support.
- Keep a cash buffer for the months that don't go to plan.

## The takeaway

Revenue is vanity if it doesn't turn into profit, and profit is fragile if it doesn't turn into cash. Watch all three, and you'll make calmer, better decisions about growth.
