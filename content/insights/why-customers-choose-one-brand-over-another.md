Ask most businesses why customers choose them and you'll hear about quality, service, price or features. Those things matter. But they describe why a customer *stays happy*, not always why they *chose you in the first place*.

Real buying decisions are faster, messier and more human than our marketing plans assume. If we want to influence them, it helps to understand how they actually happen.

## Most decisions are made on shortcuts

Psychologists often describe two modes of thinking: one that is fast, automatic and intuitive, and one that is slow, deliberate and effortful. Daniel Kahneman's *Thinking, Fast and Slow* popularised this idea. Most everyday purchases lean heavily on the fast mode. People don't build spreadsheets to choose a coffee shop, a plumber or a software tool. They rely on mental shortcuts.

Three shortcuts show up again and again.

### 1. "Which options come to mind?"

You can't be chosen if you aren't considered. Researchers at the Ehrenberg-Bass Institute, whose work is summarised in Byron Sharp's *How Brands Grow*, describe this as *mental availability* — how easily a brand comes to mind in buying situations. Brands that are easy to recall, in the moments that matter, get chosen more often. That's why consistency matters so much: the same distinctive colours, phrases and assets, repeated over time, make you easier to remember.

### 2. "Which option feels safest?"

People are generally more motivated to avoid a bad decision than to make a perfect one. Familiarity, reviews, recommendations from people they trust, clear guarantees and professional presentation all reduce perceived risk. Often the brand that wins isn't the best — it's the one that feels least likely to disappoint.

### 3. "Which option is easiest?"

Friction kills decisions. A confusing price, a long form, a slow reply, an unclear next step — each one gives the customer a reason to postpone, and postponed decisions often never happen. Making the purchase easy is a competitive advantage in its own right.

## What this means for your marketing

**Be remembered, not just seen.** Choose a small set of distinctive brand cues — a colour, a phrase, a visual style, a sound — and use them consistently everywhere. Changing your look every few months resets the memory you've built.

**Show up in the buying moment.** Think about the situations in which people realise they need what you sell. What are they searching, asking or noticing then? Be visible in those moments, not only when it suits your content calendar.

**Reduce risk visibly.** Real testimonials (with permission), clear explanations of what happens next, transparent pricing where possible and a low-commitment first step all make choosing you feel safer.

**Remove friction ruthlessly.** Walk through your own buying journey as if you were a customer. Every unnecessary step, field or delay is a reason to leave.

**Then compete on substance.** Quality, service and value still decide whether customers come back and recommend you. Psychology gets you chosen; substance keeps you chosen.

## A useful question

Next time you're planning a campaign, add one question to the brief: *in the moment this customer is deciding, what will make us come to mind, feel safe and be easy to choose?* If the campaign doesn't help with at least one of those, it's worth rethinking.
