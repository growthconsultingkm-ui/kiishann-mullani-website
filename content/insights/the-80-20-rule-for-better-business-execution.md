The 80/20 rule — also called the Pareto principle, after the economist Vilfredo Pareto — is the observation that a small share of causes often produces a large share of effects. The exact ratio is rarely 80 and 20, and it isn't a law of nature. But as a *thinking tool*, it's remarkably useful.

In most businesses, a minority of customers generate most of the profit, a minority of products drive most of the sales, and a minority of activities produce most of the results. The rest consumes time, attention and money for comparatively little return.

## Where to look

Run the 80/20 lens over a few areas of your business. You'll need some data, but rough figures are fine.

### Customers

Which customers bring in the most profit — not just revenue? Which ones take a disproportionate amount of time, discounting or stress? Often a small group of customers is both highly profitable and easy to serve. Those are the ones to understand deeply and find more of.

### Products and services

Which offers generate most of your margin? Which ones are complex to deliver, rarely bought or barely profitable? Simplifying your range can free up surprising capacity.

### Marketing channels

Where do your best customers actually come from? Many businesses discover that one or two channels drive most of their valuable leads, while others mostly generate activity.

### Your own time

Which few activities create most of your value — the ones only you can do? Which tasks could be delegated, automated or stopped entirely?

## Turning insight into execution

Finding the vital few is the easy part. Acting on it takes discipline.

1. **Double down on the vital few.** Put more time, budget and attention into the customers, offers, channels and activities that drive results.
2. **Simplify or stop the trivial many.** Raise prices on difficult, low-margin work. Retire products nobody buys. Pause channels that don't perform.
3. **Protect focus.** Once you know what matters most, guard it from the constant pull of low-value urgency.
4. **Review regularly.** What's vital changes as the business grows. Re-run the analysis every six months or so.

## A word of caution

The 80/20 rule is a lens, not a verdict. Some low-revenue activities are strategically important — a new product in its early days, a relationship that opens doors, a channel that builds long-term brand. Use the analysis to ask better questions, then apply judgement.

## Try this today

Write down every recurring activity in your working week. Mark the three that create the most value. Then ask: what would happen if I spent twice as much time on these — and what would I need to stop doing to make that possible?
