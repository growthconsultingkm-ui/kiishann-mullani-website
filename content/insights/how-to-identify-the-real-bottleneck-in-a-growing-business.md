Picture a business as a pipe. Water flows in at one end — attention, leads, enquiries — and comes out the other as delivered work, happy customers and revenue. Somewhere along that pipe there is a narrowest point. However much you widen every other section, the flow will never exceed what that narrow point allows.

That narrow point is your bottleneck. The idea is at the heart of the *Theory of Constraints*, developed by Eliyahu Goldratt and explained in his business novel *The Goal*. It's one of the most practical ideas in business, and one of the most ignored.

## Why we fix the wrong things

Growing businesses are full of problems, and most of them are real. The trouble is that working on any problem *feels* like progress. A new website, a better CRM, a refreshed logo, a new hire in a department that's already coping — each feels productive. But if none of them touches the actual constraint, overall growth barely moves.

Common symptoms of working on the wrong thing:

- Lots of activity, little change in results.
- Each team says they're doing well, yet the business feels stuck.
- Improvements in one area simply create a queue somewhere else.

## Map the flow

Start by sketching the journey from first contact to repeat customer. Keep it simple — five to eight stages is enough. For a typical service business it might look like:

1. People discover you
2. They enquire
3. You qualify and propose
4. They buy
5. You deliver
6. They return or refer

For each stage, note roughly how much flows through it each month and where things pile up or drop out. You don't need perfect data. Rough numbers and honest observations are enough to see the shape.

## Look for the pile-up

Bottlenecks reveal themselves in a few ways:

- **Queues.** Work waits in front of one stage — proposals not sent, projects waiting to start, orders waiting to ship.
- **Steep drop-offs.** Many people enter a stage, few come out — lots of enquiries, few proposals accepted.
- **One overloaded person.** Everything routes through the founder or a single key employee.
- **Idle capacity downstream.** Delivery teams waiting for work while sales struggles — or the reverse.

Often the bottleneck is not where people expect. Owners frequently assume they need more leads, when the real constraint is how quickly they respond to the leads they already have, or how much work they can actually deliver.

## Then do the unglamorous thing

Once you've found the constraint, the approach is straightforward, if not always comfortable:

1. **Get the most out of it.** Remove distractions from the constrained stage. If the founder is the bottleneck in sales calls, take every non-essential task off their plate.
2. **Make everything else support it.** Other teams should organise their work around keeping the constraint productive, not around maximising their own output.
3. **Expand it.** Hire, automate, train, change the process — invest specifically where the constraint is.
4. **Look again.** When one bottleneck is relieved, another stage becomes the narrowest point. That's a sign of progress, not failure.

## A simple monthly habit

Once a month, ask your team one question: *where is work waiting, and where are customers dropping out?* Write down the answer. Over time, you'll build an honest picture of where growth is really being held back — and you'll spend your energy where it counts.
